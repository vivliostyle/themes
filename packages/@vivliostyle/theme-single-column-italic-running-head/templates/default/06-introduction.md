# Introduction {#ch-introduction}

## Post-Glacial Estuaries as Sediment Sinks {#sec-post-glacial-estuaries}

An estuary is, in the most economical definition, a valley that the sea has entered but not yet
filled. On the temperate shelves of the northern hemisphere the great majority of such valleys were
cut during the last glacial lowstand and drowned during the transgression that followed; they have
been accumulating sediment, more or less continuously, for some ten thousand years. The rate at
which they do so is not a matter of antiquarian interest alone. Estuarine infill governs navigable
depth, determines the extent of intertidal habitat, and modulates the exchange of carbon and
nutrients between the land and the shelf. It is also, on any reasonable projection of relative
sea-level change, the process upon which the survival of temperate saltmarsh depends.<span class="footnote">A. J. Bellenger, 'Accommodation, supply and the survival of temperate saltmarsh', _Review of Holocene Coastal Change_, 18 (2021), 203–29 (p. 211).</span>

The difficulty is that the controls upon infill are numerous and are not easily separated. Sediment
may be supplied from the catchment, from the shelf, or from the erosion of the estuary's own
margins; it may be redistributed by tidal asymmetry, by wind waves, or by the density circulation
of the salt wedge; and the accommodation into which it is placed is itself a function of relative
sea level, of isostatic adjustment, and of whatever reclamation the surrounding population has seen
fit to undertake. Studies that attend to one of these controls in isolation are numerous. Studies
that attempt to weigh them against one another, over the whole of the Holocene and within a single
system, are not.

The present dissertation is an attempt at the second kind of study. It takes a single macrotidal
estuary, instruments it for eighteen months, cores it along four transects, and asks how much of its
Holocene infill can be attributed to each of the mechanisms above.

## The Alderford Estuary {#sec-the-alderford-estuary}

The Alderford Estuary lies on the Wrensea coast, some forty kilometres north of Wrenfield. It drains
a catchment of 1,180 km<sup>2</sup> of low relief and mixed agricultural use, and discharges through
a single channel that narrows sharply at Alderford Narrows before opening into the outer bay. The
mean spring tidal range at Alderford Quay is 6.8 m; the system is therefore macrotidal by the
conventional threshold, and is among the more energetic estuaries of the Wrensea coast.<span class="footnote">Wrensea Hydrographic Office, _Tidal Constituents of the Wrensea Coast_, Technical Report WHO-2020-07 (Wrenfield: Wrensea Hydrographic Office, 2020), table 4.</span>

Three features make the estuary an unusually favourable subject. The first is the sill at Alderford
Narrows, a bedrock constriction that fixes the geometry of the tidal wave and so removes one of the
larger sources of ambiguity in the interpretation of residual transport. The second is the
completeness of the Holocene section: seismic profiling by the Marlbrook Sedimentology Panel has
shown some eleven metres of unconsolidated fill above the glacial surface, with no evidence of
regional erosional truncation. The third is documentary. The reclamation of the upper marshes
between 1804 and 1893 is recorded in the estate papers of the Alderford Commissioners in sufficient
detail that the loss of intertidal accommodation can be dated to within a decade, which provides an
independent test of any chronology derived from the sediment itself.

## Aims and Objectives {#sec-aims-and-objectives}

The aim of this dissertation is to determine the relative contribution of tidal asymmetry, external
sediment supply and accommodation change to the Holocene infill of the Alderford Estuary. Four
objectives follow from it:

1. to characterise the modern hydrodynamic regime of the estuary at sufficient temporal resolution
   to resolve the spring–neap cycle, and to compute the residual sediment flux at each of three
   stations;
2. to establish a stratigraphic framework for the Holocene fill from gravity cores, and to assign to
   each unit a depositional environment on sedimentological grounds;
3. to date that framework by radiometric and event-stratigraphic means, and to derive accumulation
   rates with quantified uncertainty; and
4. to test the resulting reconstruction against the documentary record of reclamation, and to assess
   the extent to which the conclusions may be generalised to comparable post-glacial systems.
