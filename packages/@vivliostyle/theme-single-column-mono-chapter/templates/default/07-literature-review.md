# Estuarine Sedimentation: A Review {#ch-review}

## Models of Tidal Asymmetry {#sec-models-of-tidal}

The proposition that a tidal wave propagating into a shoaling channel becomes distorted, and that the
distortion imparts a residual transport of sediment, is old enough to be uncontroversial. What
remains contested is the sign of that residual in any particular system, and the conditions under
which it may reverse. The classical treatment distinguishes two regimes. Where the intertidal
storage of the estuary is small relative to the channel volume, the flood tide is the shorter and
the more energetic, and the residual is directed landward; where intertidal storage is large, the
draining of the flats delays the ebb, the ebb becomes the more energetic, and the residual is
directed seaward.<span class="footnote">M. Ollerenshaw, _Tidal Distortion in Shallow Estuaries_ (Marlbrook: Halloway & Prine, 2016), pp. 61–78.</span>

The difficulty with this account, as Ollerenshaw observed in an influential review, is that it
treats intertidal storage as a static property of the estuary when it is in fact a function of the
very infill whose direction it is invoked to explain.<span class="footnote">M. Ollerenshaw, 'Storage, asymmetry and the problem of circularity', _Journal of Estuarine Stratigraphy_, 41 (2019), 5–24.</span> An estuary that is accumulating sediment
loses intertidal storage as its flats accrete; on the classical account it should therefore become
progressively more flood-dominant, and so accumulate faster still. The system is positively coupled,
and a model that does not represent the coupling will misattribute to external supply what is in
fact an internal feedback.

Several attempts have been made to close this loop. Fenwicke and Delacour-Ings proposed a
morphodynamic formulation in which the storage ratio is recomputed at each tidal cycle from the
evolving hypsometry, and reported that their model converged upon a stable configuration only for a
narrow band of initial conditions.<span class="footnote">S. T. Fenwicke and C. Delacour-Ings, 'A morphodynamic closure for estuarine hypsometry', _Estuarine Modelling Letters_, 7 (2020), 88–104.</span> Sarrazin's more recent treatment abandons the storage ratio
entirely in favour of a direct computation of the residual from the phase difference between the
principal semidiurnal constituent and its first overtide.<span class="footnote">P. Sarrazin, 'Overtide phase as a predictor of residual transport', _Bulletin of Tidal Geomorphology_, 33 (2022), 141–58.</span> Neither formulation has been tested
against a Holocene-length record, for the sufficient reason that few such records exist at the
required resolution.

## Chronological Control in Estuarine Sequences {#sec-chronological-control-in}

Estuarine sediments are difficult to date. Radiocarbon is compromised by the reworking of older
carbon and by a marine reservoir effect that is both large and spatially variable; luminescence
dating is compromised by incomplete bleaching in turbid water; and the annual laminations upon which
lacustrine chronologies depend are only rarely preserved. The literature has consequently converged
upon short-lived radionuclides for the last century and a half, and upon event stratigraphy for
anything older.

The <sup>210</sup>Pb method has been applied to estuarine settings since the 1970s and is now
routine, but its assumptions are more demanding than is generally acknowledged. A
constant-rate-of-supply formulation requires that the total inventory of unsupported <sup>210</sup>Pb
in the core be complete, which in an eroding or bioturbated setting it frequently is not; a
constant-initial-concentration formulation requires that the specific activity of the incoming
sediment be invariant, which in a system whose grain size is changing it cannot be. Quennell's
survey of forty-three published estuarine chronologies found that fewer than a third reported the
inventory test at all.<span class="footnote">H. Quennell, 'Inventory completeness in estuarine lead-210 chronologies: a survey', _Sedimentological Notices_, 29 (2018), 312–30 (p. 319).</span>

Where the two formulations disagree, an independent marker is indispensable.
<sup>137</sup>Cs provides one such marker in the form of the fallout maximum of 1963, and in
north-west European settings a second horizon is frequently resolvable. The present study uses both,
together with the reclamation horizon described in <a href="06-introduction.html#sec-the-alderford-estuary" data-ref="sec"></a>, and reports the three as mutually
independent constraints rather than combining them into a single age–depth model.

## The Gap Addressed by the Present Work {#sec-the-gap-addressed}

Three observations follow from the foregoing. First, the coupling between infill and tidal asymmetry
is theoretically well described but empirically untested over the relevant timescale. Second, the
chronological methods that would permit such a test are available but are seldom deployed together
in a single system. Third, the documentary record of estuarine reclamation, which offers an
independent and precisely dated perturbation to the accommodation of the system, is almost never
brought into contact with the sedimentary record.

The Alderford Estuary permits all three deficiencies to be addressed at once. It is sufficiently
energetic that the asymmetry is measurable; its Holocene section is complete; and its reclamation is
documented. The methodology by which these advantages are exploited is set out in the chapter that
follows.
