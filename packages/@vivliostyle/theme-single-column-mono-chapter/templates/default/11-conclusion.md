# Conclusion {#ch-conclusion}

## Summary of Findings {#sec-summary-of-findings}

This dissertation set out to determine the relative contribution of tidal asymmetry, external
sediment supply and accommodation change to the Holocene infill of the Alderford Estuary. Four
conclusions may be stated.

The estuary is strongly flood-dominant landward of the sill at Alderford Narrows, and the residual
sediment flux there exceeds the fluvial supply by an order of magnitude. The infill of the upper
reaches is accordingly governed by the internal redistribution of material already resident within
the system, and only secondarily by delivery from the catchment.

The rate of that infill is not constant. Mean accumulation rose from 1.4 mm a<sup>−1</sup> before
1750 to 4.1 mm a<sup>−1</sup> after 1880, and the transition is dated by three mutually independent
means — radiometric, event-stratigraphic and documentary — which agree to within 3 cm of depth. The
increase coincides with the completion of the reclamation of the upper marshes, and is consistent in
sign and timing with the morphodynamic prediction that reduced intertidal storage should increase
flood dominance.

The centimetre-scale couplets of the laminated unit preserve a spring–neap signature at an
accumulation rate of about 4 mm a<sup>−1</sup>, which is appreciably below the threshold that has
been proposed for the preservation of tidal periodicity. Independent counting of the couplets yields
an accumulation rate within uncertainty of the radiometric estimate.

Finally, the geochemical and isotopic evidence for an increased marine contribution across the
reclamation horizon is real but weak, and does not by itself discriminate between the morphodynamic
explanation and contemporaneous dredging of the approach channel.

## Directions for Further Work {#sec-directions-for-further}

The most valuable extension of this work would not be a refinement of the Alderford dataset but its
replication elsewhere. The argument advanced in <a href="10-discussion.html#ch-discussion" data-ref="chapter"></a>&nbsp;rests upon a single documented
perturbation in a single estuary; a second system, reclaimed at a different date and under different
tidal conditions, would test the mechanism far more severely than any additional coring at Alderford
could. Three candidate systems on the Wrensea coast possess both the documentary record and the
stratigraphic completeness such a test requires.

Two narrower lines also suggest themselves. The first is the extension of the chronology below the
range of unsupported <sup>210</sup>Pb, which would require a solution to the reservoir-effect problem
discussed in <a href="07-literature-review.html#sec-chronological-control-in" data-ref="sec"></a>&nbsp;and would repay the effort by resolving the internal structure of the
pre-industrial interval. The second is the direct measurement of margin erosion, at present inferred
from the sediment budget rather than observed, which repeat laser scanning of the marsh cliffs would
constrain directly.

It is a commonplace that an estuary is a valley the sea has entered but not yet filled. The
Alderford Estuary suggests that the filling is less a matter of what the rivers bring down than of
what the tide declines to take away.
