# Abstract #

**Tidal Sediment Dynamics in Post-Glacial Estuaries: A Multi-Proxy Reconstruction of Holocene
Deposition in the Alderford Estuary.** Rowan Elspeth Mallinder.

Post-glacial estuaries are among the most rapidly evolving depositional environments on the
temperate shelf, yet the relative contribution of tidal asymmetry, catchment supply and relative
sea-level change to their infill remains poorly constrained. The Alderford Estuary, a macrotidal
system on the Wrensea coast, offers an unusually complete Holocene record: its drowned valley was
excavated during the last glacial lowstand and has accumulated some eleven metres of sediment since
the marine transgression of the early Holocene.

This dissertation presents a multi-proxy reconstruction of that infill, based on eighteen months of
hydrographic monitoring and on twelve gravity cores recovered along four transects. Continuous water
level and current records are combined with grain-size distributions, geochemical indices,
stable-isotope ratios and a <sup>210</sup>Pb–<sup>137</sup>Cs chronology in order to resolve the
depositional history of the system at decadal to centennial resolution.

Three principal findings are reported. First, the estuary is strongly flood-dominant at spring
tides, with a residual sediment flux directed landward at every station save the outermost; the
asymmetry is shown to arise from the shoaling of the tidal wave over the sill at Alderford Narrows
rather than from any catchment signal. Second, mean accumulation rates rose from 1.4 mm a<sup>−1</sup>
before 1750 to 4.1 mm a<sup>−1</sup> after 1880, a threefold increase that coincides with the
documented reclamation of the upper marshes. Third, the grain-size record preserves a
centimetre-scale alternation of laminated silt and cross-laminated fine sand which is interpreted as
the signature of the spring–neap cycle, and which permits an independent check on the radiometric
chronology.

Taken together, these results argue that the Holocene infill of the Alderford Estuary has been
governed principally by the internal redistribution of sediment already resident within the system,
and only secondarily by external supply. The implications for the management of comparable
post-glacial estuaries, and for the interpretation of ancient tidal successions, are discussed in
