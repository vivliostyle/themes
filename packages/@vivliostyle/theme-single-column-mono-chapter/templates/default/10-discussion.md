# Discussion {#ch-discussion}

## Internal Redistribution and the Coupling Problem {#sec-internal-redistribution-and}

The central result of <a href="09-results.html#ch-results" data-ref="chapter"></a>&nbsp;is that the residual sediment flux at Alderford Narrows exceeds the
fluvial supply by an order of magnitude. Whatever governs the infill of this estuary, it is not the
delivery of material from the catchment. The sediment that accumulates in the upper reaches has been
imported from the outer bay or eroded from the estuary's own margins, and its accumulation is
therefore a matter of internal redistribution rather than of external supply.

This is precisely the situation that Ollerenshaw identified as circular.<span class="footnote">Ollerenshaw, 'Storage, asymmetry and the problem of circularity', pp. 9–14.</span> The estuary is
flood-dominant because its intertidal storage is small; its intertidal storage is small because it
has been accumulating sediment; and it has been accumulating sediment because it is flood-dominant.
The reclamation of the upper marshes offers a rare opportunity to break into this loop, because it
constitutes an abrupt, externally imposed and precisely dated reduction in intertidal storage. The
morphodynamic formulation of Fenwicke and Delacour-Ings predicts that such a reduction should be
followed by an increase in flood dominance and hence in accumulation rate, and that the response
should be rapid — a matter of decades rather than of centuries.<span class="footnote">Fenwicke and Delacour-Ings, 'A morphodynamic closure', fig. 6. Their model was calibrated on a mesotidal system and its extension to macrotidal conditions is not straightforward.</span>

The observed threefold increase in accumulation rate, dated to the closing decades of the nineteenth
century and coincident with the completion of the reclamation, is consistent with that prediction in
both sign and timing. It is not, however, a proof of the mechanism. An increase in the supply of
sediment from the outer bay, arising for instance from the dredging of the approach channel that
began in 1881, would produce a similar signal. The two hypotheses can be distinguished in principle
by the provenance of the accumulated material, and the geochemical results reported in <a href="09-results.html#sec-geochemical-provenance" data-ref="sec"></a>
weakly favour the first, but the discrimination is not decisive and should not be represented as
such.

## The Spring–Neap Signature and its Implications {#sec-the-spring-neap}

The couplets of Unit B provide an independent and unusually direct measure of accumulation. That
they yield 3.9 mm a<sup>−1</sup> where the radiometric methods yield 4.08 ± 0.18 mm a<sup>−1</sup>
is encouraging, but the agreement should not be overstated: the counting of couplets assumes that
none has been lost to bioturbation and that none represents anything other than a spring–neap cycle,
and neither assumption can be verified from the material itself.

The wider significance of the couplets lies elsewhere. If a spring–neap signature is preserved in a
macrotidal estuary at 4 mm a<sup>−1</sup>, then the conditions under which such signatures survive
are less restrictive than has generally been supposed, and the search for them in ancient tidal
successions may be correspondingly more hopeful. Sarrazin has argued<span class="footnote">Sarrazin, 'Overtide phase as a predictor of residual transport', p. 152.</span> that the preservation of
tidal periodicity requires accumulation rates above about 10 mm a<sup>−1</sup>; the present result
suggests that the threshold is lower, at least where bioturbation is suppressed by the rapid
alternation of substrate.

## Limitations {#sec-limitations}

Three limitations qualify the conclusions drawn above. The first is spatial: twelve cores along four
transects constrain the axial variation of the fill well and its lateral variation poorly, and the
inference that the units are laterally persistent rests upon seismic correlation rather than upon
direct sampling. The second is temporal: the radiometric chronology extends only to about 1750, and
the pre-industrial rate of 1.4 mm a<sup>−1</sup> is therefore an average over a period whose internal
structure is unresolved. The third, and the most serious, is that the entire argument rests upon a
single perturbation in a single estuary. A second system, reclaimed at a different date and under
different hydrodynamic conditions, would test the mechanism far more severely than any refinement of
the present dataset could.
