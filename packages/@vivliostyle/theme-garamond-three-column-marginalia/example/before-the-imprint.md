# Before the Imprint

## Scribes, Stationers, and the Bibliotheca

What the university read, and who supplied it, in the three centuries before it printed.

The university was three hundred and twenty-nine years old before it owned a press, and for most of
that time it did not feel the want of one. Its books came to it as books came to every foundation
of its kind: by gift, by bequest, by purchase from the trade, and — for the texts that mattered
most and could be had no other way — by copying. The Bibliotheca’s earliest accession register,
begun in 1551 and kept in the same hand for thirty-one years, records eleven manuscripts written
within the walls against four hundred and six volumes bought
or given.<span class="footnote">Register of Accessions, Bibliotheca Ashcombiensis,
vol.&nbsp;I, fol.&nbsp;1r–44v.</span> The proportion is a fair measure of how a sixteenth-century
university thought about the making of books: it was something that happened elsewhere.

What did happen within the walls was binding. The Old Almonry, a stone range on the north side of
the inner court, had been given over to a binder’s shop by 1563, and the university’s accounts pay
a bindery rent, with brief interruptions, for the next two hundred and eighty years. The room that
Harbert would later fill with type cases had been full of sewing frames and paring knives since the
reign of Elizabeth.

<aside>The bindery’s tools passed to the press in 1877 and several were still in use in the nineteen-twenties.</aside>

The copying, while it lasted, was done in the Bibliotheca itself and paid for by the sheet. Four
scribes are named in the accounts between 1551 and 1578, of whom one, a chaplain called Osbert
Mell, wrote seven of the eleven manuscripts entered in the register and was still being paid for
corrections in 1583. His hand is a competent secretary with an unusually flat _e_, and it is
possible to follow it from an unfinished commentary on the Book of Job, through two medical texts
copied for a physician of the town, to a fair copy of the university’s own statutes made in the
year the stationers’ bonds began. Nothing in the accounts suggests that anybody considered the
arrangement temporary. It ended, as such arrangements did everywhere, because the price of a
printed book fell below the price of a copied one, and it ended late at Ashcombe: the last payment
for copying is dated 1591, some seventy years after the trade had made the practice uneconomic
elsewhere.

The trade in finished books was regulated, in so far as it was regulated at all, by the stationers’
bond. From 1584 any bookseller wishing to sell within the university’s liberties gave surety to the
Curators, undertook to keep no unlicensed matter, and accepted the university’s price control on
set texts. Four bonds survive from the seventeenth century and eleven from the
eighteenth.<span class="footnote">Chest of Charters, Box&nbsp;VII, Stationers’ Bonds, 1584–1798.</span>
The system suited everybody except the students, and it produced, over two centuries, the small
close-built row of shops on Candlerow that supplied the university with its reading and, in the
1790s, with the pamphlets that first proposed a press.

There had been one earlier attempt, and its failure is instructive. In 1631 a Curator named Gervase
Ninham left the university two hundred pounds “for the erecting and furnishing of a printing house
within the precinct,” and the Board resolved to accept it. A press was bought and stood in the
Almonry for four years. No sheet printed on it has ever been
identified.<span class="footnote">Minutes of the Board of Curators, vol.&nbsp;I,
fol.&nbsp;208r; and see the note in the Bibliotheca’s hand-list of presses, 1911.</span> The
equipment was sold in 1636 and the proceeds applied to the roof of the hall. The Curators had
bought a press without securing what a press actually requires: a printer, a supply of paper, and
copy worth printing.

The eighteenth century solved none of this but changed the terms of the problem. Lecturers who
wanted their work in print printed it at their own charge, through a bookseller, and the imprint on
the title page was the bookseller’s. Forty-two such titles are known from the years 1740 to 1800,
and their production is entirely respectable; the university simply had no hand in it, took no
credit for it, and drew no revenue from it.<span class="footnote">Hand-list of Ashcombe Imprints
before 1876, nos.&nbsp;1–42.</span>

<aside>Twelve of the forty-two carry the imprint of the Candlerow house of Bourne &amp; Trickett, which also printed the 1871 pamphlets against the press.</aside>

Candlerow itself deserves a sentence, because the press displaced it and then, within a generation,
depended on it. Six shops stood in the row in 1800 and four in 1876; they sold new and second-hand
books, stationery, prints, and — in two cases — patent medicines, and three of the six also printed.
Their relations with the university were cordial, mercenary, and entirely stable for the better part
of a century. The house of Bourne &amp; Trickett, the largest of them, printed the university’s
examination papers from 1823, lost the contract to the new press in 1881, and was still binding for
the press in 1904, by which time the third generation of Tricketts had a son in the Almonry works as
an apprentice.

By the eighteen-thirties two pressures had begun to tell. The first was cost: the university
prescribed set texts that the trade printed in editions too small to be cheap, and complaints about
the price of a first-year mathematics book reached the Curators in 1834, 1839 and 1847.<span class="footnote">Minutes of the Board of Curators, vol.&nbsp;II, fol.&nbsp;96v,
fol.&nbsp;141r, fol.&nbsp;203v.</span> The second was reputation. Neighbouring foundations were
issuing scholarly editions under their own names, and the Curators, who were not immune to
comparison, began to notice that Ashcombe’s learning appeared in the world dressed in other
people’s liveries.

Neither pressure was sufficient on its own. Together, and given a generation of Curators willing to
argue in public, they produced the long quarrel of 1868 to 1876 with which the next chapter is
concerned.
