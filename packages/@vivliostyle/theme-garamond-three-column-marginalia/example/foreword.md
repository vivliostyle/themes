# Foreword

Written for the sesquicentenary by the Vice-Chancellor of the University.

A press is the slowest instrument a university owns. Lecture lists are printed and forgotten;
buildings are raised and altered beyond recognition within a generation; but a book issued under
the university’s name goes out into the world and stays there, on shelves the university will never
see, answering for the institution long after everyone who made it has gone. That is a heavy thing
to hand to eleven people over a hundred and fifty years, and it is the subject of this volume.

When the Curators sealed the founding statute in May 1876 they were not, by their own account,
doing anything remarkable. They were solving a problem of supply. The university had learning it
could not get into print at a price its scholars could pay, and it had a stone building off the Old
Almonry standing empty. The two facts were put together, a stipend was voted, and a compositor was
hired. Nothing in the minutes of that spring suggests anybody expected the arrangement to outlive
the century.

<aside>The statute of 1876 is reproduced in full, from the sealed original, in Appendix C of the second impression.</aside>

It has outlived rather more than that. The press has been through a fire, two wars, four changes of
premises, one attempted abolition, and the wholesale replacement of every physical process by which
a book is made. It has printed sermons, statutes, treatises on optics, a celebrated and unreadable
concordance, several hundred thousand examination papers, and — since the second charter of 2011 —
a growing proportion of everything it publishes in forms that have no physical existence at all. In
every one of those changes the question put to the Delegates has been the same question the
Curators asked in 1876: can the university print what it needs to print, and can it be made to pay
its own way within a decade?

The answer has usually been a qualified yes, arrived at late. Readers of the chapters that follow
will notice that the press has almost never been ahead of its trade. It came to machine composition
fifteen years after the commercial houses, to offset lithography a decade late, and to structured
markup only when a journal editor forced the issue. What it has been is stubborn. The imprint has
never been leased, never been sold, and never — despite the Committee of 1953, whose report is
discussed in Chapter <a href="rationed-years.html#paper-war-and-retrenchment" data-ref="chapter"></a>&nbsp;— been closed.

A reader who comes to this book expecting a procession of famous titles will be disappointed, and
should be. The catalogue of a university press is not where most of its work is. For the first
eighty years of its existence the greater part of what came off the Ashcombe machines was
examination papers, calendars, matriculation forms, lecture lists, the annual statutes, and the
prescribed texts that undergraduates bought because they had to; the scholarly monographs that give
the press its name in the world were, in any given year before 1955, a minority of the sheets and
almost never the difference between solvency and deficit. Histories of presses are usually written
from the catalogue. This one has been written, so far as the records allow, from the day-book, and
it is the better and the duller for it.

<aside>The press printed the university’s examination papers continuously from 1881 to 1983, the longest unbroken commission in its history.</aside>

There are three things the volume cannot tell the reader, and the contributors have been careful to
say so where the gaps fall. The wage books before 1901 are lost. The correspondence of the
Delegates was weeded twice, in 1938 and again in 1969, on principles nobody recorded. And of the
women who worked in the reading room and the warehouse between the wars — who were, by 1943, rather
more than half the staff — we know the names of eleven and the work of two. A house that kept a
vellum register of every apprentice for seventy years kept nothing comparable for the people who
read its proofs.

Dr Sarre and her contributors have written a history and not a celebration, and I am grateful to
them for it. The reader will find the fire of 1908 given its proper weight, and the retrenchment of
the nineteen-fifties described without softening. A house that cannot print an honest account of
itself has no business printing anyone else’s.

It is customary in a foreword of this kind to say something about the future, and I shall resist
it. The press has been told four times in its history — in 1876, in 1908, in 1953, and in 2009 —
what it would have to become in order to survive, and on each occasion the prediction was
substantially wrong and the press survived anyway, by attending to the next twelve months. A house
that has printed continuously for a hundred and fifty years has done so not by anticipating change
but by being small enough to absorb it and stubborn enough to outlast the people who were certain
about it. I commend that method to my successors with more confidence than I commend anything else
in this volume.

To the eleven holders of the office, to the compositors, readers, warehousemen, editors, designers
and coders whose names do not appear in the appendices, and to the authors who trusted a small
provincial imprint with work that could have gone anywhere: the university’s thanks, and mine.

Hester Prendergast, Vice-Chancellor. Ashcombe, Candlemas 2026.
