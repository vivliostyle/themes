# The Digital Transition

## From Hot Metal to Markup

The fifteen years in which the press stopped thinking of a book as a set of pages.

Margaret Ledgard took over in 1990 with the composition of the list in the hands of a filmsetting
bureau, the printing in the hands of two trade houses, and the editorial department still marking up
typescripts in the pencil conventions Prowse had codified in 1938.<span class="footnote">_Rules of the House_, 4th edn (Ashcombe: for the Delegates, 1938);
in use, with amendments, until 1996.</span> The press had changed how its books were made without
changing how it described them, and the gap was becoming expensive: every correction after the
bureau’s first proof was paid for twice, once in fees and once in the fortnight it cost.

The pressure to close the gap came, as such pressure usually does, from the journals. The editor of
the _Ashcombe Review of Historical Method_ wanted an annual cumulative index and a machine-readable
back file, and in 1992 he declined to renew the division’s composition contract until he got
them.<span class="footnote">Minutes of the Journals Board, 14&nbsp;May 1992; and see Ledgard’s
note to the Delegates of 3&nbsp;June 1992.</span> The division’s answer — keying the text once,
into a described structure, and generating both the printed page and the index from it — was
adopted for the three journals in 1993, extended to the reference list in 1996, and made the house
standard for all new work in 1999.

<aside>The first Ashcombe title composed entirely from structured files was the fourth edition of the county place-name dictionary, 1994.</aside>

The intellectual shift was larger than the technical one. Under the old conventions an editor marked
a manuscript to tell a compositor what to do: this line in small capitals, this word in italic, this
paragraph set in from both sides. Under the new ones an editor marked it to say what the thing was:
a heading, a title, a quotation. What it should look like became a separate question, answerable
once for a whole series rather than once for every page, and the house style ceased to be a set of
instructions and became a set of definitions. The _Rules of the House_ were rewritten twice in six
years, and the sixth edition of 1998 is nearly twice the length of the fifth and contains almost no
typographic instruction at
all.<span class="footnote">_Rules of the House_, 6th edn (Ashcombe: for the Delegates, 1998),
editor’s preface.</span>

<aside>The fifth edition is still consulted for the press’s spelling and punctuation, which the sixth edition removed to a separate hand-list.</aside>

The house did not enjoy the transition. The two years after 1993 produced more editorial complaint
than any period since the narrowing of the measure in 1941, and for a comparable reason: a
convention that had been carried in people’s heads for half a century had to be written down, and
writing it down exposed how much of it had never been consistent. Three of the four senior
copy-editors could produce, on request, a rule for the setting of a quoted verse; no two of the
three rules agreed; and none of them was the rule in the fifth edition. The description had to be
settled before the encoding could be, and settling it took eleven meetings.

<aside>The record of those meetings, kept by the youngest of the copy-editors, runs to a hundred and forty pages and is the most consulted item in the modern Press Archive.</aside>

There was a compensation, and it arrived sooner than anyone expected. Once the description was
settled, a great deal of work that had been done by eye could be done by rule, and done once. The
index of the place-name dictionary, which had taken two people five months in 1979, was generated
in an afternoon in 1994 and then corrected by hand for six weeks — which is not the saving the
prospectus promised, but is a saving, and the six weeks were spent on the errors that mattered
rather than on the alphabet.

It also made possible the two things the press did next. The first was the retrospective conversion
of the back list: between 1997 and 2004 the press had some eleven hundred of its own out-of-print
titles scanned, corrected, and re-encoded, at a cost that the Delegates recorded at the time as
indefensible and by 2010 as the best money the house ever spent. The second was print on demand,
adopted for the converted titles in 2002, which returned to sale a list that had been out of print
for an average of nineteen years and which has never since gone out of print
again.<span class="footnote">Ledgard, _Report on the Retrospective Conversion Programme, 1997–2004_
(Ashcombe: for the Delegates, 2004), tables&nbsp;1–3.</span>

What the period did not settle, and what the press has argued about ever since, is what a book is
when it is described rather than set. The sixth edition of the _Rules_ is confident that the
distinction between structure and appearance is clean. It is not, and every house that has tried to
hold it has discovered the same awkward residue: the poem whose indentation is the sense, the
critical apparatus whose position on the page is an argument, the table that means something
different when it breaks. Ashcombe’s practice, arrived at gradually and never written down, has
been to describe what can be described and to keep a hand-set exception list for what cannot. The
list stood at forty-one items in 1999 and at nineteen in 2024, which is either progress or the
slow abandonment of the difficult cases, depending on which of the two surviving copy-editors of
that generation is asked.

Ledgard retired in 2003 — the last Director to have begun her career in a house that set type — and
the editorial department moved that year out of Gattersby Road and back into the Old Almonry, a
hundred and twenty-seven years after Harbert had carried his case of pica type up the same stair.
The building had been a lecture room, a store, and for eleven years a bicycle shed. The Delegates
minuted the return without comment, which was probably the right way to do it.

## Open Access and the Second Charter

The statute of 1876 rewritten, and a list of which four titles in five are now given away.

Peter Anselm Roe, appointed in 2003, spent his first two years on a question the press had never had
to answer: what it meant to publish something that could be copied without cost. The journals
division had been selling electronic access since 1998 on terms borrowed from the print
subscription, and the terms were failing in both directions — libraries paying twice for the same
volume, and authors increasingly required by their funders to make work freely available that the
press had contracted to sell.<span class="footnote">Report of the Journals Board to the Delegates,
9&nbsp;November 2005, §§&nbsp;3–11.</span>

The press moved earlier than most houses of its size and later than it likes to remember. Two
journals went to open access on an article charge in 2008, the third in 2010, and the monograph
list followed under a mixed arrangement from 2013. What made the change possible was not the
technology, which was by then unremarkable, but the accounts: the retrospective conversion
programme of 1997–2004 had already separated, for every title, the cost of making the text from the
cost of delivering it, and only the second of these fell when a book was given
away.<span class="footnote">Farrant, _The Cost of a Free Book_ (Ashcombe: for the Delegates, 2016),
§§&nbsp;2–5.</span>

<aside>The article charge was waived from the outset for authors without institutional funding, a provision the Delegates have twice declined to review.</aside>

The statute could not survive it. The instrument of 1876 gave the university leave “to print,
publish, and vend,” and by 2009 the press’s counsel was advising that a house which increasingly
did the second without the third was operating outside the plain sense of its own charter. The
second charter, sealed on 9 May 2011 — a hundred and thirty-five years to the day after the first —
replaced the three verbs with a single object, empowering the university “to publish, by any means
and in any form, works for the advancement of learning, and to make them available on such terms,
including without charge, as the Delegates shall from time to time
determine.”<span class="footnote">Statute of the University Press, 2011, cl.&nbsp;2; and see the
comparative table in Appendix&nbsp;C.</span>

<aside>The first charter was returned to the muniment room after the sealing and is displayed, once a year, in the Bibliotheca’s founders’ case.</aside>

The drafting took two years and turned, in the end, on a single word. The Delegates’ committee
wished to write “to publish”; counsel advised that without an object the clause would be read as
narrowly as the first charter had been, and proposed “works for the advancement of learning”; and a
minority of the committee objected that the phrase was the one the university had used in 1876 to
mean books, and would be read in 2050 to mean whatever it then meant. The minority lost and was
almost certainly right. The clause has already been argued over twice, in 2017 and 2023, and on
both occasions the argument was about what counts as a work.

<aside>The 2023 question concerned a dataset of eleven million place-name citations, which the Delegates resolved to publish and the Curators’ counsel declined to call a work.</aside>

Nadia Farrant, Director from 2012, inherited the new statute and spent seven years finding out what
it cost. The house moved in 2014 from Gattersby Road to the Ninham Building on the north campus,
consolidating editorial, journals, and the digital archive on one floor for the first time since 1983. The list grew, the proportion of it available without charge grew faster, and the balance of
formats shifted in the way set out in <a href="#tbl-form-of-issue" data-ref="tbl"></a>: print-only publication, which was ninety-four
per cent of the list in 1990, is now under fourteen per
cent.<span class="footnote">Annual Returns of the Press to the Curators, 2016–2025.</span>

Imogen Braithe, appointed in 2019 and the eleventh holder of the office, has the sesquicentenary and
its awkward arithmetic. The press published two hundred and seventy titles in the biennium
2024–2025 against thirty-one in 1938; it employs fifty-four people against five in 1900; and it
prints, itself, nothing at all. Whether the house that Harbert opened in two low rooms off the Old
Almonry and the house that occupies the second floor of the Ninham Building are the same
institution is a question this volume has deliberately left to its readers. What can be said is
that the answer the Curators gave in 1876 — that the imprint should belong to the university and to
no one else — has not yet been reversed, and that nobody has proposed reversing it.
