# The Office of Printer

Eleven holders in a hundred and fifty years, and what they were appointed to do.

The office created by the statute of 1876 has been held by eleven people, which is fewer than the
university has had Vice-Chancellors in the same period and very much fewer than it has had Bursars.
The average tenure is thirteen years and seven months. Of the ten tenures that have ended, two
holders left the office for another post, six retired from it, and two died in service; and one,
Ferrand, was persuaded to stay eleven months beyond his retiring age to see the press through the
paper shortage of 1919.

The title changed once. Until 1961 the holder was Printer to the University, a style taken directly
from the statute and describing, accurately enough, someone who was expected to be a working
craftsman. From 1961 the holder has been Director of the Press. The reconstitution minute of that
year is careful to say that the change of title implied no change in the duties, which was true at
the time and had ceased to be true within a decade.

<aside>Marchant, the first Director, was also the last holder to have set type professionally before appointment; Ledgard had done so as a trainee but never for a living.</aside>

Appointment has always rested with the Delegates, subject to confirmation by the Curators, and the
Curators have never declined a nomination. Eight of the eleven were serving members of the press’s
own staff at the time of appointment. The three appointed from outside the house — Harbert,
Cousens, and Roe — arrived at three of the four moments when the Delegates had concluded that
something had to change; the fourth produced Prowse, who was on the staff but had come to it as a
proof-reader and not as a printer.<span class="footnote">Minutes of the Delegates of the Press, appointment papers,
vols.&nbsp;I, VII, VIII, XI, XV, and XXI.</span>

Two of the eleven were dismissed from consideration at the time of appointment on grounds the
Delegates recorded and would not now record: a candidate in 1901 because he was a member of a
religious body then thought incompatible with the university’s work, and a candidate in 1934
because she was married. The second of these is the more remarkable, since the person appointed
instead was also a woman, and the minute makes no attempt to reconcile the two facts. The Delegates
struck the marriage provision from the conditions of the office in 1946, eleven years before the
university struck it from its own.

The figures in <a href="#tbl-printers-and-directors" data-ref="tbl-appendix"></a>&nbsp;count titles issued during each tenure and should be read with two
cautions. Before 1926 the press counted a pamphlet and a three-volume edition alike as one title;
after 2013 a work issued in print and in digital form is counted once, but a work issued digitally
in two versions is counted twice. The totals are nevertheless comparable within each half of the
table, and the shape they describe — a house of tens becoming a house of hundreds somewhere in the
nineteen-sixties — is not an artefact of the counting.
