# The Press Proposed

## The Pamphlet War and the Delegates’ Report

Eight years of argument, in print, over whether a university ought to print.

The proposal that became the statute of 1876 was first put to the Curators on 4 November 1868, in a
paper of eleven pages by the Reader in Moral Philosophy, Josiah Ledwell. It asked for a press on
three grounds — cheapness, permanence, and credit — and it asked for it in terms so moderate that
the Board referred it to a committee without a division.<span class="footnote">Ledwell,
_A Paper Touching the Establishment of a Printing House_ (Ashcombe: privately printed, 1868); Minutes
of the Board of Curators, vol.&nbsp;II, fol.&nbsp;318r.</span> The committee reported eighteen
months later that the thing was desirable but not yet practicable, which in the usage of the period
meant that it would be considered again when somebody insisted.

Somebody did. Between 1871 and 1874 no fewer than nineteen pamphlets on the question were printed
in Ashcombe, eleven for and eight against, and the quarrel spilled into the county papers for the
better part of two winters.<span class="footnote">Hand-list of Ashcombe Imprints before 1876,
nos.&nbsp;51–69.</span>

<aside>Twelve of the nineteen were printed by Bourne &amp; Trickett of Candlerow, who thereby took a comfortable profit from both sides of an argument about their own displacement.</aside>

The case against is worth setting out, because it was not foolish. The opponents argued that a
university press would compete with a trade that already served the university adequately; that it
would require a permanent subvention which would fall on the teaching funds; that no institution
of Ashcombe’s size could keep a printer in constant work; and that the university would find itself,
sooner or later, in the position of having to refuse to print the work of one of its own members,
with all the ill feeling that must follow.

The last of these objections was the only one its authors did not live to see vindicated. The
refusal came in 1893, and the ill feeling lasted a decade.<span class="footnote">See
<a href="academic-history.html#the-almonry-works-and-the-apprentice-book" data-ref="sec"></a>&nbsp;below, and _Report of the Committee on the University Press_ (Ashcombe, 1953),
appendix&nbsp;A, where the episode is rehearsed at length.</span>

The case for rested on figures. Ledwell’s second pamphlet, published in the spring of 1872, set out
the cost of the twelve prescribed texts as bought from the trade against their estimated cost if
printed in the university’s own house, and arrived at a saving of rather more than a third — a
figure that later proved optimistic by about half, though not so optimistic as to overturn the
argument.<span class="footnote">Ledwell, _The Cost of Books to the Student: A Second Paper_
(Ashcombe, 1872), tables&nbsp;A–C.</span> More persuasive, in the event, was the point about
permanence. A trade printer keeps standing type only so long as it pays him; a university press
could be instructed to keep a book in print because the book ought to be in print. That argument
took hold among the very Curators who had been most reluctant to vote money.

<aside>Ledwell died in 1875 and never saw the statute. The Vane Reading Room preserves his corrected proof of the second pamphlet.</aside>

Neither side, it should be said, was arguing about scholarship. Read at this distance the nineteen
pamphlets are almost entirely about money, and the two or three that raise the question of what a
university press might be _for_ do so in a paragraph and then return to the estimates. The single
exception is the eleventh, an anonymous sheet of four pages printed in December 1872, which argues
that the university’s obligation is not to print cheaply but to print what would otherwise not be
printed, and which proposes — a quarter of a century before any press adopted such a thing — a
standing fund to carry titles that could not repay their costs. Its author has never been
identified. The Delegates rediscovered the pamphlet in 1953, in the middle of the committee that
nearly closed the press, and quoted it twice.

The county papers took the quarrel up in the winter of 1872 and gave it rather more space than it
deserved, chiefly because one of the opponents was a magistrate and another kept the largest of the
Candlerow shops. Three letters in that correspondence are by Curators writing under initials, which
was thought at the time to be a decent fiction and was not; and two of the three, having argued in
public against the press in 1872, voted for it in 1875 without troubling to explain themselves.

The decisive intervention came from outside the argument altogether. In November 1874 the widow of
a former Curator, Charlotte Ninham — a descendant, as the Board did not fail to note, of the
Gervase Ninham whose bequest of 1631 had come to nothing — offered the university one thousand
pounds towards a printing house, on the single condition that the press be established within three
years or the money returned.<span class="footnote">Chest of Charters, Box&nbsp;III, Deed of Gift,
17 November 1874.</span> The condition did what eight years of pamphlets had not: it put a date on
the question.

The Delegates’ Report of March 1875 is the founding document of the press in everything but law.
Drawn up by a committee of five under the chairmanship of the Delegate for Learning, it recommended
a press on a deliberately small scale — one press, one printer, one apprentice — and it settled the
three points that the later history of the house would turn upon. The imprint was to belong to the
university. The office of Printer was to be salaried and not tenanted. And the choice of what to
print was to rest with a standing body of Delegates and not with the
Printer.<span class="footnote">_Report of the Delegates Appointed to Consider the Establishment of
a Printing House_ (Ashcombe, 1875), §§&nbsp;4, 9, 14.</span>

The Board adopted the report in June, spent nine months over the drafting, and sealed the statute on
9 May 1876. Charlotte Ninham’s thousand pounds was accepted with four months to spare.
