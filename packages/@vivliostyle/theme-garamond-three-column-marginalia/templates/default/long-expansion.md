# The Long Expansion

## Offset, Overseas, and the Paperback

Thirty years in which the press grew faster than at any time before or since, and stopped printing.

The title of the office changed in 1961. Selina Marchant, appointed in succession to Cousens, was
the first holder to be styled Director of the Press rather than Printer to the University, and the
change was not cosmetic: the Delegates had concluded that the person who chose the books and the
person who made them need no longer be the same
person.<span class="footnote">Minutes of the Delegates of the Press, vol.&nbsp;XI, 4&nbsp;May 1961,
with the memorandum on the reconstitution of the office.</span> Within a decade the press would
stop making most of them altogether.

Three decisions between 1964 and 1972 account for almost all of the growth shown in <a href="chronology.html#fig-output-by-decade" data-ref="fig-appendix"></a>.
The first was the paperback. Marchant proposed in 1964 that the press issue its own scholarly
monographs in a cheap paper-covered series after three years in cloth, a practice then unusual
outside the largest houses; the Delegates approved twelve titles as an experiment, and by 1970 the
series carried a hundred and four and was returning more than the cloth
editions.<span class="footnote">Minutes of the Delegates of the Press, vol.&nbsp;XI,
9&nbsp;July 1964; Sales Ledger, series&nbsp;P, 1964–1971.</span>

<aside>The paper covers were designed in the house and used a single ruled band and the pressmark; the format survived, with two revisions, until 2009.</aside>

The second was overseas representation. The press had sold abroad since the eighteen-nineties, always
through agents and always badly; a standing arrangement with a distributor on the other side of the
Atlantic, concluded in 1967, took export sales from eleven per cent of turnover to thirty-eight per
cent in six years and changed what the Delegates were willing to
commission.<span class="footnote">Accounts of the Press, 1966–1974, series&nbsp;E.</span> Books
that could not repay their costs from a domestic academic market of a few hundred copies could
repay them from two markets.

The third was the journals. The press had printed the university’s two learned periodicals since
1903 without publishing them; in 1972 it took both over outright, added a third the following year,
and constituted a journals division with its own staff and its own
accounts.<span class="footnote">Minutes of the Delegates of the Press, vol.&nbsp;XIII,
2&nbsp;February 1972.</span> The reasoning was cash flow rather than scholarship — subscriptions
are paid in advance and books are not — but the effect on the press’s standing was larger than
anyone predicted. By 1990 the division carried nine titles and produced rather more than half the
surplus of the whole house.

The three decisions were connected, and Marchant said so at the time. A paperback needs a market
larger than a domestic one to be worth the setting; a market larger than a domestic one needs a
distributor; and a distributor needs a list large and regular enough to be worth handling, which the
journals supplied. Each of the three would have been marginal on its own. Taken together they took
the press from a house of thirty new titles a year to a house of ninety in eleven years, and took
its staff from twenty-six to a hundred and four.

<aside>Marchant’s note of 1963, proposing all three measures on a single sheet, is the shortest document to have changed the press and is reproduced in facsimile in Appendix E.</aside>

Growth of that order changed what the press was, and not everyone welcomed it. The Delegates’
minutes for the early nineteen-seventies contain a recurring and never-resolved argument about
whether a press that now published more work from outside the university than from within it was
still the university’s press in any sense the statute would recognise. The proportion crossed fifty
per cent in 1969 and reached seventy-one per cent by 1985. Marchant’s answer — that the statute
spoke of the advancement of learning and not of the advancement of Ashcombe — was accepted, but it
was accepted the way a family accepts a decision it has stopped arguing about, and it surfaced
again in 2009 when the second charter was drafted.

Against this the machine room declined. The flat-bed machines were replaced by offset lithography in
1966; the move to a single-storey works on Gattersby Road in 1968 was made to accommodate the new
plant; and within four years it was clear that a works sized for the university’s own printing could
not be sized economically for a publisher selling in two continents. Hot-metal composition ceased
in 1979. Douglas Kerrich, who succeeded Marchant in 1978, put the case to the Delegates in his first
year and again in his fourth, and in 1983 the university closed the machine room and contracted its
printing to the trade for the first time since 1876.<span class="footnote">Kerrich, _Memorandum on the Machine Room_ (Ashcombe, 1982); Minutes of
the Board of Curators, vol.&nbsp;XXII, 15&nbsp;March 1983.</span>

<aside>Nineteen of the twenty-two machine-room staff were redeployed within the university; the two remaining pressmen finished their working lives on the proofing press at Gattersby Road.</aside>

It is tempting to read 1983 as the end of something, and the trade papers of the time read it that
way. The press itself did not. The statute of 1876 had given the university leave to print, publish,
and vend; the Delegates took the view, in a minute of some length, that the three verbs had never
been inseparable, and that a house which had given up the first in order to do more of the second
and third was doing what it had been founded to do. The output figures for the decade after 1983,
which are the highest in the press’s history to that date, are not evidence that they were right,
but they are not nothing.
