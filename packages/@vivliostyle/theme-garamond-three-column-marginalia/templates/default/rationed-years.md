# Paper, War, and Retrenchment

## The Rationed Years

Depression, paper control, and the first woman to hold the office of Printer.

The two decades after the semicentenary were the only period in which the press seriously contracted,
and the contraction had almost nothing to do with the quality of its work. It had to do with paper.

Josiah Vane, who succeeded Ferrand in 1919 and held the office until his death in 1934, ran the
house through the first of the two squeezes. Prices for the medium and demy stocks the press used
most rose by a third between 1920 and 1922, fell back, and rose again after 1929; the Delegates,
whose grant was fixed in cash and not in sheets, responded by shortening runs, then by shortening
books, and finally — in the retrenchment of 1931 — by suspending the two least profitable series
altogether.<span class="footnote">Minutes of the Delegates of the Press, vol.&nbsp;VII,
5&nbsp;March 1931; Accounts of the Printing Office, 1929–1933.</span> Output fell from a peak of
thirty-four new titles in 1928 to nineteen in 1933.

<aside>Vane’s manuscript costings, in a series of pocket-books now in the Vane Reading Room, are the fullest record of pre-war production costs to survive from any provincial press.</aside>

Vane died at the works, in the machine room, on a Tuesday morning in November 1934. The Delegates
appointed as his successor the head reader, Edith Prowse, who had come to the press as a proof-reader
in 1912, had run the reading room through the war of 1914, and had in practice been costing and
scheduling the whole of the list since 1929. She was the first woman to hold the office of Printer
in the press’s history and the third of its holders to have been trained inside the
house.<span class="footnote">Minutes of the Delegates of the Press, vol.&nbsp;VII,
19&nbsp;December 1934.</span>

Her thirteen years contain the hardest of the press’s history. Paper came under control in 1940;
allocations were made against a base period the press had spent in retrenchment, so that Ashcombe
entered the war with an allowance calculated from its worst years. Prowse’s first response was
arithmetical. She reset the standard measure of the octavo series two ems narrower, moved three
series from demy to crown, and had the whole of the prescribed-text list recomposed to a tighter
specification, saving — by her own reckoning, and the Delegates checked it — the equivalent of
eleven tons of paper over the war
years.<span class="footnote">Prowse, _Note on the Economy of the Page_ (Ashcombe: for the
Delegates, 1941).</span>

<aside>The narrowed measure was never restored. What began as a wartime economy is still the basis of the house octavo.</aside>

Her second response was editorial, and more contentious. She proposed, and the Delegates accepted,
that the press should print nothing during the war that could not be justified either as teaching
matter or as work that would be lost if it were not printed then. The effect was to reduce the list
to a third of its size and to raise, sharply, the proportion of it that sold. The figures are set
out in <a href="#tbl-output-and-paper" data-ref="tbl"></a>.

It was not popular. Four authors withdrew work rather than accept the narrowed measure, two of them
in writing and one of them at length; the Delegates’ files for 1941 and 1942 contain a
correspondence with a professor of mathematics on the setting of displayed formulae that runs to
nineteen letters and ends, as such correspondences do, with both parties conceding the point they
had never disputed. Prowse’s own view, recorded in a single line of the Diary, was that “a book
that is set is a book; a book that is not set is a manuscript.”

The press ended the war smaller, solvent, and — for the first time since 1876 — with a waiting list.
Fourteen approved titles stood unprinted in 1946 for want of paper, some of them for three
years.<span class="footnote">Minutes of the Delegates of the Press, vol.&nbsp;VIII, 8&nbsp;October
1946, appendix.</span> Prowse retired in 1947, at sixty-three, and was persuaded to stay a further
eight months to see the last of them through the machine. The Delegates’ minute of thanks is
unusually long and unusually specific; it credits her with having “kept the house at work by
keeping it small,” which is exactly what she did and not at all what she had been appointed to
do.<span class="footnote">Minutes of the Delegates of the Press, vol.&nbsp;VIII,
14&nbsp;January 1948.</span>

## The Committee of 1953

An inquiry into whether the university still needed a press, and the answer it did not expect.

Alfred Cousens came to the press in 1947 from the trade — the first Printer since Harbert to have
been trained outside the house — and inherited a going concern with a waiting list, an ageing
machine room, and a Bursar who had begun to ask, in writing, why a university of eleven thousand
students was carrying a printing works at all.

The question was formalised in February 1953, when the Curators appointed a committee of five to
consider “whether the functions of the University Press might be more economically discharged by
contract with the trade.” Its terms of reference were not neutral, and everyone understood them.
The press had run a deficit in six of the previous eight years; commercial houses were quoting
against its work and undercutting it; and the argument that had founded the press in 1876 — that
the trade would not print what the university needed at a price it could pay — was, in the
conditions of 1953, simply no longer
true.<span class="footnote">_Report of the Committee on the University Press_ (Ashcombe, 1953),
terms of reference and §§&nbsp;1–4.</span>

<aside>The committee’s papers were closed for fifty years and opened in 2003. They show three of the five members expecting to recommend closure when they began.</aside>

The press had, in fairness, given the Bursar grounds. Cousens had spent his first five years
rebuilding a machine room that had taken no capital since 1929, and had done it out of revenue,
which is to say out of the deficit; the list had grown by a third in the same period; and the
warehouse at Fennel Row was carrying stock of forty thousand volumes, a good deal of which was
never going to move. The committee’s first sitting established all of this within an hour and
appeared, on the evidence of its own minutes, to regard the remaining three sittings as a
formality.

Cousens gave evidence over four sittings and did not defend the deficit. He conceded the costings,
conceded that a commercial house could print the prescribed texts more cheaply, and conceded that
the machine room was ten years behind. What he disputed was the arithmetic of the list. Of the
hundred and ninety-one titles the press had issued since 1946, he showed, sixty-one had been
declined by at least one commercial publisher before coming to Ashcombe, and of those sixty-one,
nine were now prescribed reading in universities other than their
own.<span class="footnote">Evidence of A. Cousens, 11 and 25&nbsp;June 1953, in _Report of the
Committee on the University Press_, appendix&nbsp;B.</span> A press that printed only what the
trade would print, he argued, would be a printing office and not a press, and the university would
be paying a smaller sum for a smaller thing.

The turn came in the fourth sitting, and it came from the dissenting member. The Professor of
Economic History, who had been appointed to the committee as its most sceptical member and who
alone voted at the end for closure, put to Cousens a question the papers show nobody had thought to
ask: what proportion of the press’s output would a commercial house have declined outright, as
against merely declining to print it as cheaply? Cousens did not know, asked for a fortnight, and
came back with a figure of thirty-two per cent that the committee spent two sittings trying and
failing to dispute. It was, on any reading, the number that saved the press, and it was produced at
the request of the one man who wanted it closed.

<aside>The dissenting member’s minority note of five pages is the best short account of the case against a university press ever written at Ashcombe, and the Delegates reprinted it in 1981.</aside>

The committee reported in November against closure, by four votes to one, and recommended instead a
capital grant for the machine room, a five-year subvention on a declining scale, and the
appointment of a full-time editorial secretary to the Delegates — the first professional editor the
press had ever employed. All three recommendations were adopted. The declining subvention expired,
on schedule, in 1959, and the press did not ask for another for
twenty-two years.<span class="footnote">Minutes of the Board of Curators, vol.&nbsp;XIV,
27&nbsp;November 1953; Accounts of the Press, 1954–1981.</span>

<aside>The editorial secretaryship of 1954 is the direct ancestor of the present commissioning department, and its first holder, Selina Marchant, became Director in 1961.</aside>

The editorial secretaryship changed the house more than the capital grant did. Until 1954 the
Delegates had chosen the books and the Printer had made them, and everything between those two
operations — the reading of typescripts, the negotiation with authors, the estimating, the
scheduling — had been done by whoever had time. The first secretary took all of it, and within
three years the press had a list planned two years ahead, a standard contract, and, for the first
time, a written account of what it would and would not publish. None of these things is
interesting. All of them are the reason the output figures for the nineteen-sixties look the way
they do.

What the committee had actually done was to force the press to say what it was for, and the answer
shaped the next thirty years. Cousens’s remaining seven years went into a deliberate policy of
long, slow, unprofitable books that no one else would undertake: the county record volumes, the
critical edition of the Ashcombe chronicles, and the work for which the house is still best known
outside the university, the three-volume _Concordance to the Prose of the Fourteenth Century_,
begun in 1949, printed a sheet at a time as funds allowed, and completed in 1958 at a recorded loss
of four thousand two hundred
pounds.<span class="footnote">Accounts of the Press, 1949–1958, series&nbsp;C; and see the
Delegates’ note on the Concordance, 3&nbsp;October 1958.</span> It has never been superseded, it is
cited in every serious study of the period, and by every commercial measure the committee of 1953
would have recognised, it should never have been printed at all.
